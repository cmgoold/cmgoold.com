'Not yet, Daughter of Eve', he said. 'Not yet. But you are growing more like it. And soon, very soon, before you are on old man and an old woman, great nations in your world will be ruled by tyrants who care no more for joy and justice and mercy than the Empress Jadis. — The Magician's Nephew (C. S. Lewis, 1955)

Ironically, I read these lines in C. S. Lewis's Narnia prelude days after announcements that top AI firms are not taking enough responsibility to avoid the deployment of rogue, super-intelligent agents, of uttering their own deplorable word. Discussions about AI rage on, whether it will be net beneficial or maladaptive, if it will wipe us out entirely before 2030. It reminds me somewhat of 2020 when it suddenly dawned on everyone (well, mostly everyone) that we were part of something that wasn't fully in our control, that life was going to change for the foreseeable. Mathematical modellers might call this a phase transition, a bifurcation or shift of attitudes towards AI that occurs suddenly, non-linearly, a position that is, moreover, increasingly difficult to revert. Modellers have a name for the latter too, hysteresis, which we might more colloquially refer to as once-you-know-you-know.

Parking existential crises for a moment, I'm neither anti-AI nor fanatical about incorporating it into my life. I'm not thrilled about its load on our environment, its lack of regulation, or what it means for social relationships, and I'm sceptical that we'll ever, as Mike highlights, get the day off work. I remain cognisant of its benefits, at least in the sense of what it could achieve, new technologies making our lives easier, ignoring the nefarious actors behind the scenes. And while I interact with AI and LLMs very lightly outside work, as it generally doesn't enrich my day-to-day life, if I was still studying or if I was job searching or perhaps if I had a medical condition that was aided by AI powered tools, I would probably need to use them more.

No, I'm not neither a doomsayer not evangelist. Things are too in flux for such reductive statements. Indeed, in my professional role, AI has gone from technology I interacted with occasionally to something that I interact with daily. AI harnesses and coding tools, like Claude Code and Codex, are a core part of many engineering teams' lives, generating thousands of lines of code, reviewing code, creating tickets, opening PRs, detecting bugs, and writing documentation. AI has undoubtedly made many aspects of my professional role easier. Take linting, for example: under deadline pressure, the last thing you want to be doing is scouring your code for the remaining type-check failure, or manually running your test suite. AI tools within web apps, despite the many cringe-worthy chat bots that abound, are solving customer's pain-staking tasks, such as tedious data ingestion, and note taking.

Let's step back a little, however, and consider why those things might be useful. First, good software encompasses a range of criteria: a satisfactory user experience, a robust and safe implementation, an ease of maintenance, the ability to clearly reason about the system. Implementation speed is helpful, but is not the be-all-and-end-all factor in producing good software. And even if it was, my experience with AI coding tools is that the time spent unpicking, querying, challenging, re-instructing and re-engineering LLM outputs, especially in real, longer-term projects is not trivial. There's occasions recently where I've often wondered how much further along a project is than if we'd done it all ourselves.

Second, writing software is an embodied process, there's a feedback loop between you thinking of a solution, the code you write and debug, write and debug, and coming up with even better solutions. I write my shopping-list in my phone because I can't remember everything I need, and writing it in my phone reminds me of other things I need to do that day. I offload some of the toil of remembering to a phone app, or a piece of paper.

I'm less certain about what AI-assisted software development means for software products in the long run. I don't necessarily believe that the immediate efficiency and productivity gains provided by AI are always the most important things to optimise.

Good software encompasses a range of criteria: a satisfactory user experience, a robust and safe implementation, an ease of maintenance, the ability to clearly reason about the system. Studies comparing AI-assisted development to controls have legitimate criticisms, but they don't particularly fill me with confidence either. The comprehension debt inflicted on developers by using AI is, I think, a real risk to its long-term benefits for software development, as do code regressions and disruption to team collaboration (e.g. see Mohamed et al. 2025 for one overview). The open-source, in-development browser Ladybird recently decided to refuse pull-requests from the public, not because they don't use AI themselves (they do), but because the practical challenges involved in vetting and managing pull requests from AI-assisted developers is so unwieldy.

As large language models emit predictions from statistical models, there will always be some regression towards the mean, a preference for choosing the response with the highest likelihood. In other words, the path of least resistance. Web engine searches do this too, of course. I used to live in a place called Ski in Norway: try googling "Ski Norway" or "Ski Norway town" and the majority of articles will be about skiing in Norway. This isn't a bug, it is the best response possible on average, for the majority, conditional on the model. Prior to AI, taking the path of least resistance wouldn't be a gleaming accolade of any engineer, but because it's so fast, we make exceptions. In my early-experience using AI coding tools in longer-term projects, no matter how many skills or plans or loops or tests or documentation you create, AI implementations still run a high risk of code regressions, errors in interpretation, and a lack of consistency and coherency between different sections of a codebase, as the project grows in size. You might say that human-implemented code is exactly the same, and you'd be right, but at least you understand the code you write (most of the time). The time spent unpicking, querying, challenging and re-instructing the AI in these longer-term efforts is not trivial, and after it's all said and done, I often wonder how much further along we actually are than if we'd done it all ourselves.

I recognise my own bias here, as I've long been suspicious of anything touted as a productivity hack due to the efficiency cost entailed by changing the way you work and learning something new. I prefer the familiar over the new because, in the long run, those familiar patterns of working will lead to more time to focus on what matters, rather than constant change in habits that lead to procrastinating more. I prefer Vim, for instance, over some fancy IDE because I know Vim and know it won't change and know I'll always be able to access it, and that matters for my long term productivity. At least I think it does. This is not an argument to resist change, it's an argument to recognize when change is worth it.

Part of the art of software development and coding is that the more you work through a task, writing and reviewing code, creating documentation, implementing tests, the more you gain clarity about the problem you're solving. Cognition is embodied, in other words, and writing is critical thinking in action. AI sceptics often highlight that they miss this aspect of their job because they simply love to code, and AI evangelists say they're speaking nonsense because the point of coding for professional reasons is to create software, not to enjoy coding. But a practical middle ground is lost in these extremes, which is that writing code generates ideas, ideas that can be massaged by and discussed with AI if you wish, which is knowledge that can be used by you, the human, to amend and review your plan, enriching your own understanding, before progressing or starting the cycle again. Recently, I've been using AI much more consciously in this manner, sometimes scaffolding what I believe the implementation should look like first in code before letting AI tools fill in the gaps. How we maintain this embodied process in an AI prompting world is not obvious. Whether the effects of losing it completely are really detrimental as pondered is yet to be seen.
