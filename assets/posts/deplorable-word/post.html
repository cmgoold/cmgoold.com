'But we're not quite as bad as that world, are we, Aslan?'

'Not yet, Daughter of Eve', he said. 'Not yet. But you are growing more like it. It is not certain that some wicked one of your race will not find out a secret as evil as the Deplorable Word and use it to destroy all living things. And soon, very soon, before you are on old man and an old woman, great nations in your world will be ruled by tyrants who care no more for joy and justice and mercy than the Empress Jadis. Let your world beware. That is the warning.'

— The Magician's Nephew (C. S. Lewis, 1955)

I read these lines, somewhat ironically, in C. S. Lewis's Narnia prelude days after announcements that top AI firms are not taking enough responsibility to avoid the deployment of rogue, super-intelligent agents. Discussions about AI rage on, whether it will be net beneficial or maladaptive, if AGI will wipe us out entirely before 2030, whether anything the AI companies tell us is true or just attempts to increase their stock market appeal. It reminds me somewhat of 2020 when it dawned on everyone that we were part of something that wasn't fully in our control, that life was going to change for the foreseeable. Mathematical modellers might call this a phase transition, the point where the landscape of attitudes towards AI bifurcates into new stable states, such as strongly anti- or pro-AI stances. Unlike 2020, however, we have few theoretical models to make predictions, compared to the SIR-family of models churning out daily $\mathcal{R}$ numbers we all got used to during the pandemic.

Still, there are a lot of people writing about AI. I'm conscious of adding unnecessarily to the noise, and I've deliberated over writing anything at all. Writing about AI is hard, lest you be lumped either as a doomsayer or an evangelist, virtue signaller or LLM sycophant. Existential crises aside, which I hope we're all concerned about, many of us occupy the middle-ground, enthusiastic or at least open to the benefits of certain AI capabilities and troubled about others. Possibly more the latter than the former, at the moment. To me, we don't have enough information yet to make any clear predictions. There is a lot to be both mad and in awe about. The only thing we can really be certain of is our uncertainty of about what the future looks like.

I'm at risk of being overly passive here. There are things that are objectively not great about AI, which are worth protecting. I'm not thrilled about AI's load on our environment, its lack of regulation, the unmitigated and inexorable scraping of personal data and intellectual property, or what it implies for education, literacy, creativity and social relationships. I am sceptical that we'll ever, as Mike highlights, get the day off work. That's quite the list, but I remain cognisant of its benefits too, at least in the sense of what it could achieve, the adoption of new technologies that might make our lives easier and solve some real problems of the 21st century. Although, you might have to squint a bit at these optimistic visions of the future to ignore the nefarious actors profiting behind the scenes. While I interact with AI and LLMs very lightly outside work, as it generally doesn't enrich my day-to-day life, if I was still studying or if I was job searching or perhaps if I had a medical condition that was aided by AI powered tools, I would probably use them more.

One thing I am sure about is that I won't use AI to draft, edit, proof-read nor write posts on this blog. Why anyone would want to read that in the first place, I don't know. I also have no intention to use AI to help me write any of the code or solve any technical details I might post about, or even help maintain this site. The latter is harder to commit to because there will be genuine cases where AI is helpful, like when I'm in the throes of deciphering another Let's Encrypt certificate renewal error. Avoiding consuming AI is also increasingly difficult when bots permeate the internet and every search engine returns an AI overview. Nonetheless, using AI here is antithetical to why I have a personal blog. This site is a space for me to practice writing and some web development, record my thoughts and consolidate some of what I've learned. Writing is thinking, and thinking without LLMs is something I want to protect. My incentives are not egoless, of course. I have a website because I want a public record of my work and interests, my knowledge and capabilities, although I do not share these posts nor track visitor or reader analytics. Feigned humbleness or not, I still don't want a website that looks designed by Claude nor posts written in LLM-ease.

Avoiding the use of AI for this personal website does not mean I don't use AI. In my professional life, I use it daily, as do most other engineering teams around the world in 2026. Software engineering culture has gone through a few AI-led phase transitions in the last couple of years, bringing new ways of working that allow us to produce more, faster. Is more better? I don't know yet. More is certainly different (Anderson, 1958), but whether it's better depends not just on the how fast software is developed but its quality too, including its resilience, ease of maintenance and value to users.

On one hand, human cognition is embodied, meaning we don't think, feel or behave without interacting with, and off-loading some of those functions to, our environment (one of my favourite articles on this is Barrett, 2012: Why behaviourism isn't Satanism). Using AI to help with complicated or tedious tasks is a natural extension of this embodiment. I write a weekly shopping list because I can't remember everything I need, and I use a linter because I don't want to manually typecheck my code. This is partly to take the path of least resistance, but there is an important feedback loop too. Understanding a pile of linting errors could highlight some larger issues in the code requiring a redesign or refactor. Writing a shopping list, even a hurried one, makes planning meals and budgeting easier. You might think I'm telling you that AI is 'just a tool', but I will not patronise you. Interacting with an AI agent, even for rote tasks, is a markedly different experience to a deterministic linter. However, to me, it's still important to remember that we're off-loading cognitive tasks regularly, and AI can significantly help with that.

The other side of this is that off-loading cognitive tasks makes us more dependent on those brain-body-environment interactions. My memory might be better if I devised strategies to keep my shopping list completely in memory. My knowledge of strong typed languages would probably be better if I checked types manually, as I code. It's a question of balance, of deciding which skills we want to protect and which can be commissioned elsewhere. With AI, the balance has shifted entirely, and certain cognitive tasks have gone beyond an embodied cognitive process to a fully-automated process, the path of zero resistance. Before 2026, this would never had been a glowing attribute of any software engineer, or engineering process.

Off-loading most, if not all, of the software development life-cycle to AI might make sense when it genuinely makes us all more productive and builds better products. It turns out, however, we don't have a firm sense of what productivity means in an AI-first world, or we're only just collecting enough data to track it. I'm usually sceptical of any 'productivity hack' as the time spent changing how I normally do things infrequently results in procrastinating and getting sidetracked more. Similarly, a non-trivial amount of time developing with LLMs is spent (and costs incurred) unpicking, querying, challenging, formulating, re-instructing and re-engineering LLM outputs, especially in real, longer-term projects. Accelerated implementation speed and breadth of feature development are just two components of software development, and neither guarantees good software. James Shore writes that AI coding tools need to reduce software maintenance at the same rate they increase feature development to make us more productive. Studies comparing AI-assisted development to controls have legitimate criticisms, but they don't particularly fill me with confidence either. The comprehension debt inflicted on developers by using AI is a risk to its long-term benefits in software development, as are the frequency of code regressions and disruption to team collaboration (e.g. see Mohamed et al. 2025 for one overview). The open-source, in-development browser Ladybird recently decided to refuse pull-requests from the public, not because they don't use AI themselves (they do), but because the practical challenges involved in vetting and managing pull requests from AI-assisted developers is so unwieldy. Of course, these issues might not be hurdles for very long.

The cultural shifts in software engineering are permeating many other domains too. AI is creating a bottleneck of slop in various sectors. I'm not telling you how to interact with AI. My concern is that if AI doesn't increase productivity and doesn't produce better software and doesn't make our lives easier, then what's it all for? Or, more realistically, who is it all for?
