I read my first Paul Auster novel, The New York Trilogy, in mid-2024 after receiving it in my monthly, second-hand book subscription box. Paul Auster died in April 2024 and the always-prudent Pat from the Gently Used Book Company sent The New York Trilogy in that month’s box in commemoration of Auster’s most well-known work. Since then, he’s become one of my favourite authors.

I’ve always read books, but it’s taken a couple of years of consistently reading fiction again to begin to understand what makes certain authors good writers to me. I don’t know about you, but I get a lot of pleasure out of the simple act of reading itself, following the bare rhythm of the words and how each sentence and paragraph connect to each other. I often find myself picking up a book and reading a few sentences, not necessarily taking in any of the narrative or dialogue, only how the sentences flow. Paul Auster wrote long, sometimes stream-of-consciousness sentences, some comprising a whole paragraph, but they never feel weary to read or like they are overdoing a literary device. He managed to shunt the narrative forward with each additional sub-clause of a sentence, explaining a little more in each one. For instance, here’s an example from Auster’s last published work, Baumgartner (2023), after the main, elderly character has fallen down some stairs:

“He has no idea that his limbs are moving, but he nevertheless knows that he is still conscious, for a number of disconnected thoughts are bouncing around in his head, even if those thoughts are dim and incomprehensible to him, which would disqualify them as true thoughts, he supposes, and relegate them to the category of almost-thoughts or non-thoughts, except perhaps that in spite of the pain assaulting his elbows and right knee, there is no pain in his head, which would suggest that his skull has survived the fall without any serious knocks, which in turn would suggest that when all is said and done the accident will not have turned him into a blithering, drooling idiot ripe for the glue factory.”

—Baumbgartner (2023, p.14)

From interviews I’ve read and watched with him, I know he wrote slowly and methodically — a single page of A4 was apparently a good day’s writing for him — and believed that the paragraph was the basic unit of composition. The rhythm of prose were important to him, and that’s one reason why I enjoy his writing so much. I know this type of writing is not for everyone, however.

Beyond Auster’s writing style, his fascination with chance and uncertainty has always appealed to my interests in statistics. His plots and themes often entailed, sometimes hinged on, apparently-random, coincidental, ironical events, which could shape a character’s life in an instant. Strangers happening to meet twice at exactly the same place and time of day, a character dying from the same causes and on the same day as their father, meeting their life-long partner on a street that shares a name with the street they grew up on. These are examples I invented, but they are in the spirit of coincidences he’d write about. And it wasn’t just within his books that these events occurred, but across his books, too, where character names, street names, and anecdotes and situations would re-appear. I don’t think this was to create some world of make-believe or impossibility but because Auster was a realist and revelled in the completely natural, if highly improbable. Auster said, in a 1988 interview in Bomb, that “I believe the world is filled with strange events. Reality is a great deal more mysterious than we ever give it credit for.”.

In The Invention of Solitude (1982), his first non-fiction, quasi-autobiographical work, he explained some of his thinking on the differences between causality and meaning in fiction and the real world:

“In a work of fiction, one assumes there is a conscious mind behind the words on the page. In the presence of happenings, in the so-called real world, one assumes nothing. The made-up story consists entirely of meanings, whereas the story of fact is devoid of any significance beyond itself.”

—The Invention of Solitude (1982, p.146)

As a post-modernist writer, this lack of inherent meaning in completely natural, real-world events became a cogent literary device for Auster’s novels. From the opening of City of Glass, part of the The New York Trilogy:

“It was a wrong number that started it, the telephone ringing three times in the dead of night, and the voice on the other end asking for someone he was not. Much later, when he was able to think about the things that happened to him, he would conclude that nothing was real except chance. But that was much later. In the beginning, there was simply the event and its consequences.”

—City of Glass (1987, p. 1)

In the same 1988 Bomb article referenced above, Auster explained that the opening of City of Glass was based on a true story:

“The opening scene in the book is something that actually happened to me. I was living alone at the time, and one night the telephone rang and the person on the other end asked for the Pinkerton Detective Agency. I told him that he had the wrong number, of course, but the same person called back the next night with the same question. When I hung up the phone the second time, I asked myself what would have happened if I had said, ‘Yes.’ That was the genesis of the book, and I went on from there.”

—Bomb (1988)

That the real world can generate some astonishing coincidences allows Auster to use the inversion of causality to highlight distress or dismay in his characters. For instance, from Moon Palace:

“Causality was no longer the hidden demiurge that ruled the universe: down was up, the last was the first, the end was the beginning. Heraclitus had been resurrected from his dung heap, and what we had to show us was the simplest of truths: reality was a yo-yo, change was the only constant.”

—Moon Palace (1989, p.62)

Perhaps the most obvious example of Auster’s interest in chance and uncertainty is in the aptly-titled 1990 novel The Music of Chance, which would make a great statistics book title:

“For one whole year he did nothing but drive, traveling back and forth across America as he waited for the money to run out [...] Three days into the thirteenth month, he met up with a kid who called himself Jackpot. It was one of those random, accidental encounters that seem to materialize out of thin air — a twig that breaks off in the wind and suddenly lands at your feet. Had it occurred at any other moment, it is doubtful that Nashe would have opened his mouth. [...] It all came down to a question of sequence, the order of events.”

— The Music of Chance (1989, p.2)

Finally, a last example from 4, 3, 2, 1 (2016), probably my favourite Auster novel to date, starts with the story of Isaac Reznikoff, a Russian Jew arriving as an immigrant in the United States on the first day of the twentieth century, speaking no English:

“The man said to him: Forget the name Reznikoff. It won’t do you any good here. You need an American name for your new life in America, something with a good American ring to it [...] Tell them your Rockefeller, the man said [...] [B]y the time the nineteen-year-old Reznikoff sat down to be questioned by the immigration official, he had forgotten the name the man had told him to give [...] Slapping his head in frustration, the weary immigrant blurted out in Yiddish, Ich hob fargessen (I’ve forgotten)! And so it was that Isaac Reznikoff began his new life in America as Ichabod Ferguson.”

— 4, 3, 2, 1 (2016, p.1)

That such a whimsical encounter could define a whole lineage is the music of chance in operation.

It is perhaps no surprise that Auster was intrigued by chance and uncertainty given that he had personally experienced a number of life-changing events that we might call ‘unlucky’ or rare. Witnessing a boy being killed by lightning as a child at summer camp, outliving his son and grand-daughter, being in a car accident that could very well have killed him and close family members after a momentary loss of concentration, his father dying unexpectedly from a heart-attack, and discovering the family secret that his grandmother shot and killed his grandfather in what was, in turns out, a high profile trial in Wisconsin. Auster only learned about the latter after his cousin met a stranger on an aeroplane from the same Wisconsin town who recognised the last name and sent local newspaper clippings of the trial coverage. He writes about these events in his autobiographical works.

Recently, I had my own moment of coincidence while visiting the beautiful town of Rothenberg ob de Tauber, Germany, of all places. I had just finished reading Candide, or Optimism by Voltaire (1759), a book that I had wanted to read due to Paul Auster’s references to it in a few of his books. The next day, sat right in the centre of the local bookshop’s (Buchhandlung Rupprecht GmbH) English language section was Ghost Stories (2026) by Siri Husvedt, the memoir of her life lived alongside and married to Paul Auster, including caring for him during his long illness with cancer and subsequent death. I spent the next few days engrossed in this touching book, as we travelled around southern Germany.

Siri Husvedt covers a lot in Ghost Stories about her and Paul’s life together, including his draw towards the coincidental. “Paul didn’t believe in fate” Husvedt writes, “He believed accidents changed lives”. She mentions that Auster referred to these accidents as “the mechanics of reality”, a more technical, academic phrase than ‘the music of chance’.

Paul Auster wrote about coincidences and happenstance, and Siri Husvedt’s writing is similarly influenced by the strange but real workings of the brain and cognition. She is not a doctor or academic, but has long been fascinated by neuroscience and engaged in its academic culture. As she wrote in The Shaking Woman or A History of My Nerves:

“What began with curiosity about the mysteries of my own nervous system had developed into an overriding passion. Intellectual curiosity about one’s own illness is certainly born of a desire for mastery. If I couldn’t cure myself, perhaps I could at least begin to understand myself.”

—The Shaking Woman... (2011, p.6)

Husvedt’s books frequently reference the curiosities of the nervous system and brain, including how loose the notions of I, the self, and other really are, the problems of subjective experience. In What I loved, the narrator tells a story about mistaking their reflection for another person, determining that “recognition is far more feeble than we suppose” (What I loved, 2011, p. 254). Auster used similar themes in his books, a sign of his post-modernist leanings, like in City of Glass, where the main character Quinn writes under a pseudonym William Wilson, and Quinn “treated him with deference, at times even admiration, but he never went so far as to believe that he and William Wilson were the same man”. Auster frequently played with the roles of reader and author, witness and actor.

It would be lazy to infer that Husvedt’s writing was simply inspired by Auster. I believe you can see both of their influences working in tandem, and can imagine Auster and Husvedt mutually fascinated by the workings of reality. In Ghost Stories, Husvedt remarks on the merging of self and other when living with someone for as long as she lived with Paul, and also the loss of self when living with someone as famous as Paul Auster, including the loss of her own literary achievements in her own right.

“The what-is-it-like-to-be-married-to question not only annihilates the reality of a close marriage as a dialogue between me and you but replaces the you with a third-person him, a cultural object, Paul Auster.”

—Ghost Stories (2026, p.225)

As Husvedt comments on, it’s not simply Paul Auster and Siri Husvedt, but Paul and Siri or Paul/Siri. After reading books from each of them, admittedly more of Auster’s than Husvedt’s, I feel them merging in my mind too.

If you want to read some Paul Auster, I’d start with The New York Trilogy, probably followed by Moon Palace or Leviathan. As I said above, 4, 3, 2, 1 is my favourite but it’s a tome. If you want to try something different, read Timbuktu. For Husvedt, I enjoyed and would re-read What I loved, and The Shaking Woman, or A History of My Nerves gives more insight into her personal experiences with mental health, and her passion for neuroscience, psychiatry and psychodynamics. I can’t recommend Ghost Stories enough, but I think it is better read after getting to know each of their works a little bit.
