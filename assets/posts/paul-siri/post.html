I read my first Paul Auster novel, The New York Trilogy, in mid-2024 after receiving it in my monthly, second-hand book subscription box. Paul Auster died in the April of that year and the always-prudent Pat from the Gently Used Book Company placed The New York Trilogy in that month's box in commemoration of Auster's most well-known work.

Since then, he's become one of my favourite authors, holding a dedicated space on my bookshelf for (at the time of writing) around two-thirds of his catalogue, probably more than any other author outside of a few longer series. I'm not yet sure what makes writers good writers to me, why I find certain writers more enjoyable to read than others, but suffice to say that it's extremely difficult to put Paul Auster novels down. It's not unusual for me to peruse random books, opening them at random pages and enjoying reading for the simple act of reading, gaining a rhythmical pleasure from the structuring of sentences regardless of the narrative or plot itself, and Paul Auster novels are very satisfying reading in this regard. His long, sometimes stream-of-consciousness sentences just work for me, and I know from a few of his interviews that he took great care over the structuring of his sentences and paragraphs, often writing no more than a single page of A4 a day.

Beyond Auster's writing style, his fascination with chance and uncertainty has always appealed to my interests in statistics. His plots and themes often entailed, sometimes hinged on, apparently-random, coincidental, ironical events, which could shape a character's life in an instant. Strangers happening to meet twice at exactly the same place and time of day, a character dying from the same causes and on the same day as their father, meeting their life-long partner on a street that shares a name with the street they grew up on. I invented these instances but they are in the spirit of coincidences he'd write about. And it wasn't just within his books that these events occurred, but across his books, too. Character names, street names, anecdotes would re-appear, not to create a world of make-believe or impossibility but because he was a realist and revelled in the completely natural, if highly improbable.

Causality was no longer the hidden demiurge that ruled the universe: down was up, the last was the first, the end was the beginning. Heraclitus had been resurrected from his dung heap, and what we had to show us was the simplest of truths: reality was a yo-yo, change was the only constant.

— Moon Palace (1989, p.62)

In a work of fiction, one assumes there is a conscious mind behind the words on the page. In the presence of happenings, in the so-called real world, one assumes nothing. The made-up story consists entirely of meanings, whereas the story of fact is devoid of any significance beyond itself.

— The Invention of Solitude (1982, p.146)

Indeed, Auster personally experienced a number of life-changing events that many of us would call 'unlucky' or rare. Seeing a boy being hit and die from a lightning strike as a child at summer camp, outliving his son and grand-daughter, being in a car accident that could very well have killed him and close family members.

Auster had a number of phrases to describe the reality of coincidences, perhaps most famously 'the music of chance'. From his 1990 novel The Music of Chance:

For one whole year he did nothing but drive, traveling back and forth across America as he waited for the money to run out [...] Three days into the thirteenth month, he met up with a kid who called himself Jackpot. It was one of those random, accidental encounters that seem to materialize out of thin air — a twig that breaks off in the wind and suddenly lands at your feet. Had it occurred at any other moment, it is doubtful that Nashe would have opened his mouth. [...] It all came down to a question of sequence, the order of events.

My favourite Auster novel to date, which was his last work of fiction, 4, 3, 2, 1 (2016), starts with the story of Isaac Reznikoff, a Russian Jew arriving as an immigrant in the United States on the first day of the twentieth century, speaking no English:

The man said to him: Forget the name Reznikoff. It won't do you any good here. You need an American name for your new life in America, something with a good American ring to it [...] Tell them your Rokefeller, the man said [...] [B]y the time the nineteen-year-old Reznikoff sat down to be questioned by the immigration official, he had forgotten the name the man had told him to give...Slapping his head in frustration, the weary immigrant blurted out in Yiddish, Ich hob fargessen (I've forgotten)! And so it was that Isaac Reznikoff began his new life in America as Ichabod Ferguson.

That such a whimsical encounter could define a whole lineage is the music of chance in operation. As someone interested in statistics, this theme of chance directly appeals to me too on a professional level. Of course, what we view as chance or randomness is anything but coincidental, in that randomness is in the eye of the beholder, not a property of nature. Flipping a coin may and observing the result may feel like a 'random' choice between heads and tails, but the act of flipping abides by physical laws that are not random in any way, and someone much smarter than I could build a physical model that predicts whether heads or tails will land upwards to a pretty accurate degree.
